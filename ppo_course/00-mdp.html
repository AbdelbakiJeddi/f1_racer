From 'no labels' to a Markov Decision Process, with your car as the example.

Driving without a driving rulethe problem

You want the car to stay between two yellow lines. The classic way is to write the rule yourself: a PID on the lateral error, gains tuned by hand.

Machine learning offers a second way: supervised learning. Show the network thousands of camera frames, each labelled with the correct steering. But nobody has those labels. A human driver would have to drive every situation, including the near-crashes you most need.

What you can say is how good an outcome was: "you stayed centred", "a wheel touched the line". Reinforcement learning (RL) learns from exactly that: a score, not a correct answer.

To remember. RL replaces labels (what to do) with rewards (how good it was). Everything else in this course is about turning that weak signal into a good policy.

The MDP: five ingredientsformal model

The loop is formalised as a Markov Decision Process (MDP). Every RL algorithm, PPO included, assumes these five pieces exist, even if it never writes them down.

The key line is P: PPO never sees the physics equations. It only sees samples (s, a, r, s′). That is why it is called model-free.

To remember. PPO is model-free: it learns from samples of the dynamics, never from the equations. Your simulator could be swapped for a real car without changing the algorithm.

The Markov propertywhy the observation list matters

An MDP assumes the Markov property:

P(st+1 | st, at) = P(st+1 | st, at, st−1, at−1, …)

In words: the present contains everything needed to predict the future. History adds nothing.

Why the agent cares: the policy sees one observation and must choose one action. If two situations look identical but need different actions, no policy can handle both. Learning cannot fix missing information.

Why e_heading and the speeds are in your observation? A camera frame gives a position. Position alone is not Markov for a moving car: you also need where it is going. e_heading, vx, vy and wz are the 'derivative' information that makes the observation (nearly) Markov.
To remember. If the observation is not Markov, the policy must guess. Check your observation list first, before any tuning.

State vs observation: your car is partially observedPOMDP

The actor does not get the state. It gets a camera-derived observation. When observation ≠ state, the problem is a POMDP (Partially Observable MDP).

What is hidden from the actor in your project:

  • the exact track geometry ahead (only two scan bands of the image)
  • the throttle actuator state (rate-limited internally, only its effect vx is seen)
  • the true lateral error when no line is visible (the detector then answers from memory)

Your detector's memory buffer is a small history inside the observation function. It turns 'no line visible' into 'last known centre', which restores part of the Markov property.

Time: why 30 decisions per seconddt, decimation

Physics runs at 120 Hz (dt = 1/120 s). The policy decides at 30 Hz: each action is held for 4 physics steps (decimation = 4).

Two different needs:

  • Physics needs small steps to stay stable (wheel contacts, suspension).
  • Decisions should be as slow as the task allows. Every extra decision per second makes the episode longer in steps, so rewards that matter are more steps away.

The camera renders once per decision, which is also the most expensive part of a training iteration.

Horizon in steps = 1/(1−γ), explained on page 1. Same look-ahead in seconds needs a γ that depends on the decision rate.

Why decimation 4? The steering servo is already rate-limited to 3 rad/s (0.1 rad per decision). Deciding faster than the actuator can follow adds steps without adding control.

How an episode ends: terminated vs truncatedthe most misunderstood detail

An episode ends for one of two very different reasons:

TerminatedTruncated
meaningthe MDP really endedwe stopped watching
your termsoff_track, rollover, lap_completedtime_out (40 s)
future reward0, by definitionunknown but not 0: the car could have driven on
what the algorithm doesno future valueestimate the future with the critic: bootstrap

Mixing them up is a classic bug: if a time-out counted as a crash, a car that drives perfectly for 40 s would be punished at the end for something that is not a failure.

Isaac Lab passes the time-out flag to rsl_rl, which adds γ·V(s) to the last reward of truncated episodes. Page 2 shows why that is exactly the missing future.

Watch out: lap_completed. It is a termination, so finishing a lap ends the reward stream. With a positive reward every step, completing a lap means losing all future reward: the agent has no reason to want it. Also, lap detection lived inside the removed lap_completion reward, so in your current config this term never fires.

Why 64 cars at onceparallel environments

All 64 cars are independent copies of the same MDP. They share one policy.

  • Throughput. One GPU renders 64 cameras and simulates 64 cars per step: about 1050 samples/s in your run.
  • Decorrelation. Consecutive steps of one car are almost identical. 64 cars give 64 different situations per step, which makes each update less biased toward one moment.

Your config file defaults to 32 (F1RacerSceneCfg(num_envs=32)); the training command overrides it with --num_envs 64.

Deep dive: the formal definitions

MDP = (S, A, P, R, γ). A policy π(a|s) is a probability of each action in each state. The goal is the policy that maximises the expected discounted return J(π) = E[Σ γt rt] (page 1).

POMDP adds an observation function O(o|s). Standard fixes when the observation is not Markov: stack the last k observations, give the policy a memory (recurrent network, LSTM/GRU), or engineer features that carry the missing information. Your project uses the third option (e_heading, speeds) plus the detector memory.

In Isaac Lab, terminated and truncated follow the Gymnasium API: step() returns both flags separately so algorithms can bootstrap only on truncation.
