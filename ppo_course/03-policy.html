3 · The stochastic policy

What the actor really outputs: a bell curve over actions, and what happens between that curve and the wheels.

Why not just output a steering angle?the problem

The simplest policy is a function: observation in, action out. Same input, always the same action. It is called deterministic.

For learning, it has two flaws:

  • No exploration. It only ever tries what it already does, so it can never discover that steering a bit earlier pays more.
  • No gradient through the world. To improve, we must know how the return changes when the action changes. The simulator (physics, camera, detector) cannot be differentiated. A probability over actions can (page 4).

The Gaussian policywhat the actor really outputs

Your actor is a stochastic policy πθ(a|s): for each observation, a bell curve (Gaussian, or normal distribution) over each action.

a ∼ N( μθ(s), σ² ) μθ(s) : the mean, computed by the network from the observation σ : the standard deviation, one learned number per action

θ is the set of all actor weights (43 330 here). Training changes θ, which moves μ for every observation at once.

In your config σ is state-independent: the same spread whatever the car sees. It is a separate parameter, not a network output.

In play mode the mean is used directly: no sampling, no noise.

Why a Gaussian? Continuous actions need a continuous distribution. The Gaussian has a closed-form log-probability and entropy (both needed by PPO), one knob for 'where' (μ) and one for 'how wide' (σ), and is cheap to sample on the GPU for 64 cars at once.

Log-probability: the quantity PPO pushes onformula, then meaning

The density of one action value a under N(μ, σ²):

π(a|s) = 1/(σ√(2π)) · exp( −(a − μ)² / (2σ²) )

Its logarithm, summed over the two actions (steer and throttle are independent):

log π(a|s) = Σi [ −(ai − μi)² / (2σi²) − log σi − ½·log 2π ]

Why the log: products of probabilities over many steps become sums, numbers stay in a safe range, and the gradient is simple:

∂ log π / ∂μ = (a − μ) / σ²

Read that last line: if the sampled action a was above the mean, increasing log π means moving μ toward a. The step is larger when σ is small.

That is the whole mechanism of policy learning: after an action turned out well, move the mean toward it; after it turned out badly, move away. Page 4 derives it.

Break it: sampling and the ±1 wallinteractive

Actions are clamped to −1…1 by the environment after sampling. Push the mean near the edge and watch the samples pile up on the wall.

Why the limit exists

−1…1 maps to physical limits: steer ±0.45 rad (the knuckle's range), throttle 0…3 m/s. The network has no idea of these limits, so the environment enforces them.

The hidden cost

rsl_rl computes log π on the raw sample (e.g. 1.3), while the car executed 1.0. All samples beyond the wall have the same effect on the car but different probabilities. The mean can drift past ±1 with no visible change, and the gradient toward it comes from noise. A policy whose mean sits beyond the wall has effectively saturated.

Verified: μ = 0.9, σ = 0.2 → 30.9 % clipped. μ = 1.2 → 84.1 %. σ = 1 (rsl_rl's default init_std) at μ = 0 → 31.7 %.

Why clip_actions is null in your agent config? Isaac Lab's wrapper can clip actions before they reach the environment; with clip_actions: null it does not, and the clamp happens in your action term. Either way the policy samples unbounded values. The common alternative is a tanh 'squashing' of the sample, with a log-probability correction, as in SAC.

The action is not what the car doesactuator dynamics

Between the policy's action and the wheels, your action term adds two filters to the steering:

target = asteer × 0.45 rad filtered = 0.7·current + 0.3·target (low-pass, α = 0.3) current += clamp(filtered − current, ±0.1) (slew limit 3 rad/s × 1/30 s)

So a full-lock command needs several steps to take effect. Predict, then step through.

Why filter the steering at all? A real servo cannot jump; a policy sampled with noise σ would otherwise make the wheels shake at 30 Hz. The filters give smooth, realistic steering. The cost is lag: a full reversal needs 12 steps (0.4 s) to reach 90 %. The policy must anticipate, and the return must look at least that far ahead (γ = 0.99 does).
Why steer is in the observation? The filter has memory (the current angle). Without seeing it, the policy could not know whether its last command has arrived yet: the observation would not be Markov (page 0).

Entropy: measuring how random the policy isexploration

Entropy H measures the spread of a distribution. For a Gaussian it depends only on σ:

H = ½·log(2πe) + log σ per action

Negative values are normal: for continuous distributions, entropy is relative. Only its change matters.

PPO adds a small entropy bonus to the loss (entropy_coef = 0.008). It rewards keeping σ larger, which fights premature collapse: a policy that stops exploring before it has found the good behaviour stays stuck.

In TensorBoard, Loss/entropy and Policy/mean_std tell the same story through H = const + log σ.

Why init_std = 0.2 and not 1.0? rsl_rl's default is 1.0. In your units, σ = 1 means steering noise of ±0.45 rad (full lock either way) and a third of all actions hitting the wall: the car would leave the track before learning anything. σ = 0.2 means ±0.09 rad of steering noise and ±0.3 m/s of speed noise: enough to explore, small enough to survive.
Why there is no lower or upper limit on σ? rsl_rl's default std_range is (1e-6, 1e6): effectively unbounded. Nothing but the entropy bonus stops σ from collapsing. In your run it fell from 0.2 to 0.019 once the task was solved, which is fine then, but harmful if it happens before success.

Deep dive: other ways to parameterise the policy
  • State-dependent σ: the network also outputs σ(s), so the policy can be confident on straights and exploratory in bends. More expressive, less stable.
  • log σ instead of σ (std_type: "log"): the parameter is log σ, so σ can never become negative and changes are relative. rsl_rl supports both.
  • Squashed Gaussian: a = tanh(u), u ∼ N(μ, σ²). Actions stay inside ±1 by construction; log π gets a correction term −Σ log(1 − tanh²u).
  • Beta distribution: naturally bounded on [0, 1], used in some driving work.
