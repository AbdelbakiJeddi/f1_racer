4 · The policy gradient

How to improve a policy through a simulator you cannot differentiate.

We cannot differentiate the worldthe problem

Goal: change the actor weights θ so that the expected return J(θ) goes up. Gradient ascent needs ∂J/∂θ.

But the chain θ → action → physics, camera, detector → reward goes through a simulator we cannot differentiate. PhysX gives no gradients; even if it did, contacts make them meaningless.

The policy gradient gets the gradient anyway, without touching the simulator. The trick: differentiate the probability of the actions, which we own, not the world.

The log-derivative trickderivation, one line at a time

Start with a single decision (a 'bandit'): one action a ∼ πθ, one reward R(a).

J(θ) = Ea∼πθ[ R(a) ] = ∫ πθ(a) R(a) da ∇J = ∫ ∇πθ(a) R(a) da R does not depend on θ = ∫ πθ(a) ∇log πθ(a) R(a) da because ∇π = π·∇log π = Ea∼πθ[ R(a) · ∇log πθ(a) ]

The last line is an expectation, so we can estimate it by sampling: try N actions, then average R·∇log π.

What it means

For each sampled action, ∇log π points in the direction that makes that action more likely. Weight it by how good the action was. Good actions pull the policy toward themselves, bad ones push it away.

With the Gaussian of page 3, ∇μ log π = (a − μ)/σ². So the update of the mean is:

Δμ ∝ average of R(a) · (a − μ) / σ²

No physics equation appears anywhere. The simulator only has to return R.

To remember. Policy gradient = try actions, score them, make the well-scored ones more likely. It works through any black-box environment.

Break it: a one-step steering banditinteractive

One decision: choose a steer a. The ideal is a* = 0.1, reward R = −(a − a*)². The policy starts with μ = 0.6. Each iteration samples a batch, estimates the gradient, and moves μ.

True gradient at μ = 0.6: −2(μ − a*) = −1.0, so μ should go down.

From one action to a whole trajectorythe real case

A trajectory τ = (s₀, a₀, s₁, a₁, …) has probability

pθ(τ) = p(s₀) · Πt πθ(at|st) · P(st+1|st, at)

Take the log: products become sums. The dynamics terms P do not depend on θ, so their gradient is zero. Only the policy terms remain:

∇log pθ(τ) = Σt ∇log πθ(at|st)

This is why the unknown physics drops out: it is a fixed factor in the probability, independent of θ.

∇J = E[ Σt ∇log πθ(at|st) · Gt ]

Each action is weighted by the return from its own step onward, Gt, not the episode's total. An action cannot change rewards that came before it (causality), so including them only adds noise.

This estimator is called REINFORCE (Williams, 1992). PPO is REINFORCE plus three fixes for its noise and instability.

In your projectwhere REINFORCE hides inside PPO

rsl_rl never writes 'REINFORCE'. But look at its loss at the very first gradient step of an iteration, when the new policy still equals the old one (ratio = 1):

surrogate = −A · ratio , ratio = πθ(a|s) / πold(a|s) ∇ratio at θ = θold = ∇log πθ(a|s) ⇒ ∇surrogate = −A · ∇log πθ(a|s)

Exactly the policy gradient, with the advantage A in place of Gt, and a minus sign because optimisers minimise.

To remember. Every PPO update starts as a policy-gradient step. Everything else in PPO controls how far that step may go.
Deep dive: why ∇π = π·∇log π, and why E[∇log π] = 0

From calculus: ∇log f = ∇f / f, so ∇f = f·∇log f. Applied to πθ(a) this gives the trick.

A useful identity follows: Ea∼π[∇log π(a)] = ∫ ∇π(a) da = ∇∫ π(a) da = ∇1 = 0. The score has zero mean. So subtracting any constant b from R does not change the expected gradient:

E[ (R − b)·∇log π ] = E[ R·∇log π ] − b·0

…but it can strongly reduce the variance. With b = E[R], the batch-1 estimate in the toy problem drops from std 2.37 to 1.75, and from 51 % to 7 % wrong-sign. That is the baseline, page 5, and V(s) is the natural choice for b.
