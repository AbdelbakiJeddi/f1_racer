1 · Reward and return

The car does not maximise the next reward. It maximises the discounted sum of everything after.

One reward is not enoughthe problem

The car steers now; a wheel touches the line 25 steps later. The reward of this step says nothing about that.

We need one number that scores an action by everything that follows it. That number is the return.

heading error 0.2 rad at 2.5 m/s → drifts sideways at 0.50 m/s centre line → wheel on the line (car centre at 0.42 m): 0.85 s = 25 steps

Building the returnfrom first principles

Attempt 1: add all future rewards

Gt = rt + rt+1 + rt+2 + …

Two problems. For a car that never crashes, the sum grows without limit, so two good policies cannot be compared. And a reward 1000 steps away counts as much as the next one, though it is far less certain and far less related to the current action.

Attempt 2: discount the future

Gt = rt + γ·rt+1 + γ²·rt+2 + … , 0 ≤ γ < 1

Each step further away is multiplied once more by γ. With γ = 0.99, a reward 69 steps ahead counts half.

The recursive form (used everywhere later)

Gt = rt + γ·(rt+1 + γ·rt+2 + …) = rt + γ·Gt+1

'Return now = reward now + γ × return from the next step.' Page 2 turns this line into the Bellman equation.

Why γ = 0.99 for this car? The danger you care about (heading error growing into a line touch) unfolds over about 25 steps. Weight of a reward 25 steps ahead: γ = 0.9 → 0.07 (almost invisible), γ = 0.95 → 0.28, γ = 0.99 → 0.78 (clearly felt). Going higher (0.999) makes the critic predict 33 s ahead, where the future is mostly noise: learning gets slower and noisier for no driving benefit.

Notice that a failure is never punished explicitly in your config. The cost is the green area that is cut off: all the future that will never be collected.

To remember. Termination is a penalty in disguise: it costs the whole discounted future. That is why a car with only positive rewards still learns to avoid crashing.

Speed now dominates. At 3 m/s, progress gives 6 of the 7.5 points per step. Centering gives at most 1. The return will prefer a fast car that is roughly centred over a slow, perfectly centred one.

The trained run you analysed used a different set (centering 3.5, no progress): returns of ≈ 13, and no reason to drive faster than 1.5 m/s.

Why multiply by dt? It makes the return independent of the control rate. Doubling the decision rate doubles the number of steps but halves dt, so seconds of good driving keep the same value. It also keeps values small (25, not 750), which is easier for the critic network.

Positive or negative rewards: it changes the behavioursign matters

Mostly positive per step

Every extra step alive pays. Ending the episode loses future reward. Behaviour: avoid ending. Risk: stall or crawl if survival alone pays enough.

Mostly negative per step

Every step costs. Ending early stops the costs. Behaviour: the agent learns to crash fast. This is why "−1 per step" designs need a big explicit failure penalty.

Your config is mostly positive (centering, progress, heading). Failing costs the future (up to 25), so the termination itself does the job of a crash penalty.

Deep dive: the objective PPO maximises

The quantity PPO tries to increase is the expected discounted return from the start:

J(π) = Eτ∼π [ Σt γt rt ]

The expectation is over trajectories τ = (s₀, a₀, r₀, s₁, …) produced by running π in the environment: random because the policy samples actions and the world may be random. Two policies are compared by this single number.

Why use γ < 1 even though episodes end after 1200 steps? Without it, the critic would have to predict up to 1200 steps of reward, and the time left in the episode would change values of otherwise identical states. The discount keeps the problem 'local' and time-independent.

Alternatives exist (average-reward RL, finite-horizon returns), but discounted returns are what PPO, rsl_rl and nearly all robotics RL use.
